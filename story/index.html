This is Aarav, 17, in Class 12. He has snoozed his alarm three times.

8:15 am

Lights off at 11:30. Asleep at 2:10.

He did try. But the moment the lights went off, his brain switched on: the mock test, the question he got wrong, what Papa would say. Around one he picked up his phone “just for a minute”. The next time he looked, it was 2:10.

Now he’s on the school bus, reading the same line of chemistry notes for the fourth time. Worry kept him up, and today the tiredness will make every worry hit harder. Nobody on the bus can see any of that. They see a boy yawning into his notes.

The day ahead looks normal: school till two, coaching till seven, and forty unread messages in the family group already. Boards are four months away, and every relative he meets says this is the most important year of his life.

1:30 pm

Laughing at lunch. Counting the minutes until it’s over.

Someone reads out the mock test ranks from the class group. Aarav laughs in the right places and makes the joke everyone expects from him. Last night’s stories showed half his class at a birthday he didn’t know about. Nobody mentions it, so neither does he.

Nobody at the table would guess. On the bad days he’s usually at his funniest. Ask anyone there and they’d tell you he seems fine.

4:50 pm

Then the thoughts get loud.

In the auto to coaching, he opens Instagram. A cousin’s IIT admit letter. A classmate’s 5 am “study with me” reel. In the family group, Papa has forwarded a topper’s interview with the caption “Very inspiring!!”

What if I don’t get in. Everyone’s so far ahead. They’ve spent so much on my coaching. Think a worry enough times and it starts to sound like a fact. Half an hour of reels into an auto ride, it’s hard to tell the two apart.

untangle one of these thoughts nobody posts their bad mock test.

7:10 pm

A message from a friend: “you okay?”

Dinner is dal, the TV news, and Mummy asking about the mock test before he’s even sat down. He says “it was fine” and looks at his plate. Later, his phone buzzes. It’s Kabir from coaching: “you okay? you were really quiet today.”

His thumbs type “haha yeah just tired”. He looks at it for a long time. Then he deletes it and writes: “not really, honestly.” He presses send before he can change his mind.

9:30 pm

Talking didn’t fix everything. It made it shareable.

Turns out Kabir hasn’t slept properly in weeks either. They send voice notes for an hour, half complaining, half laughing. Later, Aarav finds his mum in the kitchen and tells her, quietly, that things have been really hard. She doesn’t have the perfect words. She warms two glasses of haldi doodh and sits with him anyway.

Nothing is solved. The boards are still four months away. But Kabir wants to revise together on Sunday, and Mummy hasn’t asked about the mock test once since. Small things, but they count.
