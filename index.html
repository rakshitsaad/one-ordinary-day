This is Aarav, 17, in Class 12. He has snoozed his alarm three times.

8:15 am

Lights off at 11:30. Asleep at 2:10.

He did try. But the moment the lights went off, his brain switched on: the mock test, the question he got wrong, what Papa would say. Around one he picked up his phone “just for a minute”. The next time he looked, it was 2:10.

Now he’s on the school bus, reading the same line of chemistry notes for the fourth time. Worry kept him up, and today the tiredness will make every worry hit harder. Nobody on the bus can see any of that. They see a boy yawning into his notes.

The day ahead looks normal: school till two, coaching till seven, and forty unread messages in the family group already. Boards are four months away, and every relative he meets says this is the most important year of his life.

1:30 pm

Laughing at lunch. Counting the minutes until it’s over.

Someone reads out the mock test ranks from the class group. Aarav laughs in the right places and makes the joke everyone expects from him. Last night’s stories showed half his class at a birthday he didn’t know about. Nobody mentions it, so neither does he.

Nobody at the table would guess. On the bad days he’s usually at his funniest. Ask anyone there and they’d tell you he seems fine.

4:50 pm

Then the thoughts get loud.

In the auto to coaching, he opens Instagram. A cousin’s IIT admit letter. A classmate’s 5 am “study with me” reel. In the family group, Papa has forwarded a topper’s interview with the caption “Very inspiring!!”

What if I don’t get in. Everyone’s so far ahead. They’ve spent so much on my coaching. Think a worry enough times and it starts to sound like a fact. Half an hour of reels into an auto ride, it’s hard to tell the two apart.

7:10 pm

A message from a friend: “you okay?”

Dinner is dal, the TV news, and Mummy asking about the mock test before he’s even sat down. He says “it was fine” and looks at his plate. Later, his phone buzzes. It’s Kabir from coaching: “you okay? you were really quiet today.”

His thumbs type “haha yeah just tired”. He looks at it for a long time. Then he deletes it and writes: “not really, honestly.” He presses send before he can change his mind.

9:30 pm

Talking didn’t fix everything. It made it shareable.

Turns out Kabir hasn’t slept properly in weeks either. They send voice notes for an hour, half complaining, half laughing. Later, Aarav finds his mum in the kitchen and tells her, quietly, that things have been really hard. She doesn’t have the perfect words. She warms two glasses of haldi doodh and sits with him anyway.

Nothing is solved. The boards are still four months away. But Kabir wants to revise together on Sunday, and Mummy hasn’t asked about the mock test once since. Small things, but they count.

One sense at a time.

Go slowly. Write things down if it helps, or just notice them. Both count.

5 things you can see

Look around slowly. Big things, tiny things, a colour, a shadow. Let your eyes land and stay for a moment.

2 of 5

4 things you can touch

Notice what’s against your skin: your clothes, the floor under your feet, something cool or soft.

3 of 5

3 things you can hear

Listen past the loudest thing. A fan, the traffic, birds, a pressure cooker somewhere, your own breath.

4 of 5

2 things you can smell

If nothing comes, that’s okay. You can remember a smell you like instead: rain on dry ground, chai, a freshly washed shirt.

5 of 5

1 thing you can taste

Maybe toothpaste, the last thing you ate, or take a sip of water and really notice it.

Myths we grew up with.

Usually from people who loved us and meant well. Tap a note to see what’s actually true.

  • myth

    “It’s just a phase.”

    tap to flip
    actually…

    Some moods do pass. But if sadness or worry hangs around for weeks and gets in the way of sleep, school or friends, it deserves real attention, not a wait-and-see. Asking early is never an overreaction.

  • myth

    “Therapy is for crazy people.”

    tap to flip
    actually…

    Therapy is for anyone carrying something heavy: exam stress, a breakup, a fight at home, a mood you can’t explain. It’s like seeing a doctor for a sprain. You don’t wait until you can’t walk.

  • myth

    “Boys don’t cry.”

    tap to flip
    actually…

    Everyone cries. Boys are usually taught to swallow it, so they carry the same feelings with nowhere to put them. Most of the men you know have cried too. They just didn’t let you see.

  • myth

    “Just think positive and it’ll go away.”

    tap to flip
    actually…

    If it worked like that, nobody would struggle. Feelings aren’t a switch you flip by trying harder. What helps is real support: someone who listens, rest, and sometimes a professional. “Just be happy” usually makes people feel more alone.

  • myth

    “What stress? You’re just a kid.”

    tap to flip
    actually…

    Ranks, comparison, friendships, expectations at home: teenage stress is real stress. Around 1 in 7 people aged 10 to 19 lives with a mental health condition (WHO, opens in a new tab). Being young doesn’t make it small.

  • myth

    “Talking about it will only make it worse.”

    tap to flip
    actually…

    Saying it out loud usually makes it lighter, not heavier. Being listened to, even once, can turn a spiral into something you can actually look at. You don’t need the perfect words. “Not really, honestly” is enough.

  • myth

    “Strong people handle it alone.”

    tap to flip
    actually…

    The people who seem strongest usually have someone they call when it gets bad. Asking for help is a skill, and like any skill, the second time is easier than the first.

  • myth

    “Sleep is for after the exams.”

    tap to flip
    actually…

    Teenagers need 8 to 10 hours of sleep a night (CDC, opens in a new tab). A tired brain makes everything harder: focus, mood, memory. Revising at 2 am feels productive; the trouble is remembering any of it the next day.
