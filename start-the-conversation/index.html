How do I start the conversation?

You don’t need a speech. A text, a note left on their desk, or bringing it up in the car, where nobody has to make eye contact: all of these count. Pick whoever feels safest, and borrow any of these words.

  • A friend
  • A parent
  • A teacher
  • A school counsellor

A friend

A good choice when you want someone your own age who gets it, and you’re not ready to tell an adult yet.

Things you could send or say

  • hey, can we talk sometime today? i’ve been feeling kind of off lately and don’t really want to deal with it alone

  • short version

    not doing great tbh. can i call you later?

  • remember you asked if i was okay? i wasn’t really. can we talk?

  • in Hinglish

    yaar, thodi der baat karein? mann theek nahi lag raha

A few tips

  • Pick a time when neither of you is rushing off to class or coaching.
  • Texting first is completely fine. It can be easier than saying it out loud.
  • If they don’t get it, that says more about them than you. One bad reaction doesn’t mean you shouldn’t try someone else.

A parent

A good choice when you feel safe at home and want them to know what’s really going on, beyond marks.

Things you could send or say

  • Mumma/Papa, I need to tell you something and I need you to just listen first. I’ve not been feeling okay for a while.

  • short version

    Can we talk tonight? It’s not about marks.

  • I’ve been finding things really hard, and I don’t know how to say it properly. Can you just sit with me for a bit?

  • in Hinglish

    Mumma, aapse kuch baat karni hai. Bas pehle sun lena, please.

A few tips

  • Choose a calm moment: not right before an exam, and not in the middle of an argument.
  • If saying it feels too hard, write it in a note or send it as a WhatsApp message first, even from the next room.
  • Worry can come out as anger or advice at first. Give it a day, or try your other parent or another adult you trust.

A teacher

A good choice when there’s a teacher you trust, or school itself is part of what feels hard.

Things you could send or say

  • Ma’am/Sir, can I talk to you for five minutes after class? I’m having a hard time with some stuff and don’t know who to ask.

  • short version

    Ma’am/Sir, do you have a minute after class?

  • I’m finding it hard to keep up, and it’s not just the studies. Is there someone at school I could talk to?

  • in Hinglish

    Ma’am, class ke baad paanch minute mil sakte hain? Kuch baat karni thi.

A few tips

  • Ask for a time when they’re not rushing to the next class.
  • You can hand them a note instead of saying it.
  • If they’re busy or brush it off, try another teacher or the school counsellor. One bad reaction doesn’t mean you shouldn’t try someone else.

A school counsellor

A good choice when you want someone trained to listen who isn’t family. This is exactly what they’re there for.

Things you could send or say

  • Hi, I’m in Class 12. I’ve been feeling stressed and low for a few weeks and I’d like to talk to someone about it.

  • short version

    Hi, can I book a time to talk?

  • I don’t know if this is a big enough reason to come, but I haven’t been sleeping well and I feel low most days.

  • in Hinglish

    Hi, aapse baat karne ka time mil sakta hai? Kuch hafton se mann theek nahi hai.

A few tips

  • You don’t need a big reason. Stress, sleep, feeling low or lonely are all things they help with.
  • Write down a few points before you go, so you can read them out if your mind goes blank.
  • You can ask what stays private before you start.

If the first person brushes it off, try someone else, or one of the free helplines. A bad first reaction doesn’t mean you were wrong to say it.

How to help a friend who’s struggling

You don’t have to fix anything. Taking them seriously is most of it.

What you can say

  • “You seemed off today. You okay?” And if they say “fine”, ask twice.
  • “That sounds really hard. I’m glad you told me.”
  • “Do you want me to just listen, or help you work out who to talk to?”
  • “Want me to sit with you while you call?”

What to avoid

  • Brushing it off: “everyone’s stressed”, “just think positive”, “you’ll be fine”.
  • Turning it into a story about you, or comparing who has it worse.
  • Promising to keep it a secret no matter what.

When to tell a trusted adult

If your friend talks about hurting themselves or not wanting to be alive, take it seriously. Tell a trusted adult, like a parent, a teacher or the school counsellor, straight away, even if they asked you to keep it a secret. It’s the right thing to do, and you can be a good friend and still tell an adult. If they’re in immediate danger, call 112.

Supporting someone can feel heavy. It’s okay to talk to someone about how you’re feeling, too.

Whenever you’re ready.
